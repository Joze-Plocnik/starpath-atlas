    How to play & atlas sources

    Identify the constellation using only its stars. Use its full Latin name, such as Ursa Major; English translations and three-letter abbreviations are not accepted. A correct name earns one point and reveals the constellation's border.

    Then type a neighboring constellation's Latin name and select Place neighbor. Click or tap inside that constellation's actual region. Correct placement earns one point and reveals its stars, border, and name. Naming alone reveals nothing. Unknown constellations stay hidden. Any point inside the named constellation counts; it need not be near the shared edge. Drag to pan, use the zoom buttons, or use arrow keys and Enter to aim. Escape cancels a pending placement.

    Classic mode: find all neighbors of the starting constellation. Each found neighbor adds its stars to the map at the selected magnitude limit. Finding every neighbor advances to a new star-only round after a short pause, keeping your score. Reveal name and Skip remaining neighbors allow practice, without points for skipped answers.

    Conquer mode: correctly identify the starting constellation to claim it, then name and place any unclaimed constellation bordering any of your claims. All claimed stars and borders remain available. Use View around to center the map on a claimed constellation and explore farther across the sky; traveling keeps claims, score, and a pending placement. There are no round resets or free reveals. Claim all 88 constellations to finish, including constellations with no stars at the selected limit. Your final territory remains available to explore.

    Each correct constellation name or successful neighboring claim earns one point. Repeated answers earn no extra points. Switching modes or selecting New game starts a fresh game and resets the score. Changing the magnitude limit keeps earned points and claims; the bundled catalog supports limits up to 5.00. Only starting patterns with qualifying stars are used.

    This celestial atlas is independent of your location and time. Borders and neighbors use CDS VI/49 (Davenhall & Leggett; adjacency data by Bill Gray), defined in B1875 and precessed to J2000 for display. Serpens's two regions are treated as one constellation.
